# Introduction

Tactile sensing provides robots with information about the local contact between an object and a sensor. Visuo-tactile sensors capture this interaction as images of a deformable sensing surface, making contact geometry and surface deformation available to image-based perception methods. GelSight demonstrates how changes in illumination and marker positions can support estimates of geometry and force (Yuan et al., 2017). Compact sensors such as DIGIT extend this sensing approach to robotic fingers and have been used for in-hand manipulation (Lambeta et al., 2020). These capabilities make visuo-tactile images a useful representation for studying how local contact information can support robotic perception and manipulation.

Learning from these images requires datasets that represent the contacts a robot may encounter. Physical acquisition must account for variations in object geometry, contact position, orientation, indentation, and sensor appearance. Collecting this variation requires repeated interactions and an acquisition procedure that associates measurements with the relevant experimental conditions. A review of visuo-tactile data generation identifies physical collection and synthetic generation as complementary approaches, with simulation providing control over conditions that can be difficult to annotate in real experiments (Sun et al., 2025). Synthetic datasets are therefore useful for investigating contact representations and training procedures, although their value depends on how well the generated variation represents the intended sensing task.

Several simulators already generate tactile images from object geometry. Gomes et al. (2021) construct GelSight-like images from depth information using an approximate deformation model and illumination-based rendering, and investigate their use in sim-to-real learning. TACTO supports mesh-based rendering, configurable sensor designs, and integration with a physics engine for large-scale synthetic data collection (S. Wang et al., 2022). Other approaches emphasize sensor-specific optical response. Taxim uses example contacts to estimate spatially varying appearance and models marker displacement separately (Si & Yuan, 2022), while FOTS combines a learned optical model with calibrated marker-motion functions for tactile-motor learning (Zhao et al., 2024). Together, these studies establish both geometric image generation and calibration from real measurements as important components of tactile simulation.

Detailed physics-based simulation can be computationally expensive because generating a tactile image may require solving elastomer deformation before optical rendering. Chen et al. (2023) identify the computational cost of finite element simulation as a motivation for Tacchi, which uses the material point method to approximate deformation. TacFlex further demonstrates that increasing the elastomer mesh density improves deformation accuracy at the expense of computation time, and reports deformation and rendering performance separately (C. Zhang et al., 2025). This cost matters when a dataset requires many distinct contact configurations. However, physics-based simulation is not uniformly slow: TacSL uses GPU parallelization to support high-throughput visuo-tactile simulation and robot learning (Akinola et al., 2025). The relevant trade-off is therefore the mechanical detail required by the task relative to its computational and modelling demands.

For generating static tactile images under prescribed contact configurations, a simpler geometric approach offers a task-specific alternative. Constructing an approximate indentation surface directly from the object mesh avoids solving the elastomer's mechanical response for each sample and removes the need to identify constitutive material parameters. Prior work supports the feasibility of this approximation: Gomes et al. (2021) generate tactile images from depth and approximate deformation for sim-to-real learning. Here, geometric simplification allows contact shape, indentation, and optical appearance to be varied explicitly within a single dataset-generation procedure. Its justification is the ability to produce controlled image variation with fewer modelling dependencies; a speed advantage over other implementations requires a matched benchmark. The resulting images remain approximations and cannot establish mechanically accurate force, shear, or slip.

The dataset-generation workflow must also connect input geometry, contact parameters, rendering settings, and exported samples in a traceable procedure. Mesh2Tact retains these conditions alongside each generated image, supporting controlled comparisons between generation settings and inspection of the resulting contact distributions. Reference-based appearance fitting complements the geometric model by adjusting the background and lighting to the supplied sensor recordings. Together, these components provide a reproducible basis for examining how geometric and optical choices affect synthetic tactile images, while keeping agreement with fitting images distinct from generalization to independent real contacts.

In this study, we present **Mesh2Tact**, a mesh-to-tactile framework for automated generation of synthetic visuo-tactile datasets. The framework integrates geometric contact-depth construction and configurable RGB rendering with seeded sampling of object orientation, lateral position, indentation depth, and image effects. Its contributions are an integrated generation pipeline that exports tactile images with geometric depth and contact-mask outputs; a configurable batch-generation procedure that records sampling settings and sample-level contact parameters; and a reference-image-based background and lighting fitting procedure for approximating the appearance of the supplied GelSight recordings. The study examines these components through generated contact examples, recorded dataset characteristics, and paired real and approximate images. The appearance comparisons characterize preliminary fitting agreement; they do not establish independent sim-to-real validation or mechanically accurate contact behaviour.
