# 2. Related Work

## A. Sensor Design and Image Formation

Generating tactile images from a mesh requires a model of how contact becomes an observable signal. Tactile perception spans geometry, texture, material properties, and pose, each requiring suitable contact representations (Luo et al., 2017). GelSight encodes surface geometry through elastomer shading and captures additional deformation through marker motion (Yuan et al., 2017). Dong et al. (2017) improve its optical design for geometry and slip measurement. DIGIT provides compact sensing for manipulation (Lambeta et al., 2020), while GelSlim emphasizes calibrated imaging and durability (Donlon et al., 2018). GelSlim 3.0 extends these capabilities to shape, force, and slip measurement (Taylor et al., 2022).

Sensor geometry and transduction also affect image formation. GelTip observes a curved surface with one camera (Gomes et al., 2020), whereas OmniTact uses multiple cameras for multidirectional sensing (Padmanabha et al., 2020). AllSight combines rounded geometry with learned contact estimation and synthetic localization data (Azulay et al., 2024). TacTip instead tracks internal pins (Ward-Cherrier et al., 2018), and DTact estimates depth from indentation-dependent darkness (C. Lin et al., 2023). DigiTac's comparison of sensor designs shows that similar pose-estimation accuracy need not imply equivalent tactile servoing performance (Lepora et al., 2022). These differences motivate defining the sensor scope before selecting a simulation model. For GelSight-style synthesis, the immediate requirements are an approximate contact surface and its optical appearance.

## B. Contact Modelling and Tactile Rendering

Geometric methods address these requirements without a detailed elastomer solve. Gomes et al. (2021) combine depth, Gaussian-based deformation approximation, and Phong illumination. Phong's (1975) empirical shading model relates appearance to surface normals but does not model contact mechanics. TACTO renders object and sensor meshes with configurable optics, synchronizes poses with a physics engine, and approximates compliance through a force-to-indentation mapping (S. Wang et al., 2022).

Calibration complements this geometry by modelling sensor-specific appearance. Taxim fits spatially varying RGB mappings and models marker displacement separately using an approximation informed by offline finite element simulation (Si & Yuan, 2022). FOTS combines a neural optical mapping with calibrated shadow and marker-motion models (Zhao et al., 2024). An alternative is to model light transport: Agarwal et al. (2021) use path tracing and inverse rendering with an approximate contact surface, while Agarwal et al. (2025) apply physically based rendering to sensor design. Sun et al. (2023) investigate efficiency-tunable path tracing through changes in surface representation. Optical fidelity and mechanical fidelity are therefore separate modelling choices.

Explicit deformation models address the mechanical component. Tacchi uses the material point method to construct the deformed surface (Chen et al., 2023), and Tacchi 2.0 adds dynamic contacts and marker-image generation (Sun et al., 2026). TacFlex combines finite element deformation with coating-pattern and optical distortion modelling (C. Zhang et al., 2025). SimTac addresses biomorphic structures through material point simulation and learned mappings informed by finite element data (X. Zhang et al., 2026). These methods support deformation phenomena beyond static geometric contact.

Their additional detail has computational implications. TacFlex reports increasing computation time with elastomer mesh density (C. Zhang et al., 2025). Nevertheless, physics-based simulation is not uniformly slow: TacSL integrates GPU-parallel compliant contact, rendering, and robot-learning tools (Akinola et al., 2025). For static image generation, a geometric model avoids per-sample elastomer solving and material-parameter identification, although comparative speed requires matched measurements. This narrower scope motivates examining how approximate contacts can be organized into useful datasets.

## C. Dataset Generation and Sim-to-Real Transfer

Dataset construction connects the rendering model to acquisition, sampling, and supervision. Existing approaches use physical, synthetic, or hybrid data (Sun et al., 2025). Calandra et al. (2018) learn grasp and regrasp decisions from physical visual and tactile observations. Li et al. (2019) learn cross-modal prediction from paired visual–tactile sequences, generating appearance from observed images rather than prescribed mesh contacts. Mesh-conditioned generation offers explicit contact control: Gomes et al. (2021) systematically sample object contacts, while TACTO demonstrates large-scale randomized grasp collection (S. Wang et al., 2022).

The generation model also determines the available supervision. Si et al. (2022) combine calibrated contact mechanics and Taxim rendering for grasp-stability prediction on real observations. Sferrazza et al. (2020) learn force estimation from simulated deformation and optical flow; their dense reference force fields associated with real images are simulation-derived. A geometric depth map should therefore be treated as a model output, rather than evidence of mechanically accurate force.

Transfer depends on both representation and sampled variation. Ding et al. (2020) randomize a simplified tactile model and evaluate transfer with marker-coordinate representations. Tactile Gym 2.0 trains policies on simulated depth and learns a translation from real tactile images into that representation (Y. Lin et al., 2022). This demonstrates a use for simplified geometry with a real-data-trained domain bridge. More generally, Tobin et al. (2017) randomize visual appearance, whereas Peng et al. (2018) randomize physical and control parameters. These address different sources of mismatch; static image variation does not reproduce contact dynamics.

Evaluation must distinguish appearance from training utility. SSIM compares local luminance, contrast, and structure (Z. Wang et al., 2004), while LPIPS compares learned visual features (R. Zhang et al., 2018). Both characterize visual similarity, not tactile mechanics. Real grasp prediction (Si et al., 2022) and manipulation-policy transfer (Akinola et al., 2025) provide additional evidence of downstream usefulness.

Mesh2Tact builds on these established methods by connecting mesh-derived contact geometry, reference-based optical fitting, and controlled sampling in one dataset-generation workflow. It exports RGB images, approximate depth, contact masks, and generation parameters so that samples remain traceable to their configurations. The study focuses on how these choices affect static contact appearance, dataset variation, and generation cost. This provides the basis for the following methodology, with fitting agreement kept distinct from independent transfer validation.
